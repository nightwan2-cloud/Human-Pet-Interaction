# Portable character and clothing guidance

These are the relevant appearance and wardrobe rules bundled from the North American rug-character workflow. No source PDFs, private paths, wardrobe library, or additional installed skill are needed.

## Identity and photographic realism

- Follow the requested ethnicity, gender, person count, relationship, and age. If age is unspecified, depict an adult without inventing a numeric age. Do not introduce children unless requested. For groups, distinguish each requested identity and outfit.
- Select a few compatible features rather than assembling a checklist. White North American adults may have varied fair, light-beige, or light-olive complexions and varied natural hair colors, textures, and facial shapes; do not narrow them to one European stereotype. Black North American adults may have varied medium-to-deep complexions and natural coils, curls, locs, braids, or other individual hairstyles; do not equate ethnicity with one complexion or hairstyle. Follow other identities when requested.
- Natural facial proportions, slight asymmetry, continuous anatomical transitions, believable face-to-body scale, subtle skin-tone variation, restrained texture and fine facial hair, and soft controlled reflectance. Makeup and grooming should suit the person and activity.
- Any freckles or marks should be sparse, faint, and localized. Avoid oily shine, plastic or waxy skin, dense speckling, exaggerated pores, artificial symmetry, beauty-ad retouching, gritty noise, oversharpening, halos, or smeared skin.
- Use a natural viewing distance within the room, not a face close-up. Do not add macro eye, iris, pupil, eyelid, or corneal details. Avoid quality-keyword stacks.
- Natural expressions and body language must respond to the actual event. Ethnicity does not determine personality, social class, or lifestyle.

## Original outfits

- Create exactly one complete original outfit per person, suitable for season, room temperature, activity, and requested formality. Compose it from garment categories, cut, fabric, pattern, and styling decisions; do not retrieve a finished outfit or merely recolor a previous one.
- Use any supplied wardrobe document to learn garment categories, proportions, construction details, textile types, and styling relationships. Independently recombine those elements for the current scene. Do not paste, closely paraphrase, fill an example's slots, or reproduce its garment-and-color combination. This portable reference contains extracted style guidance, not the complete source wardrobe catalog; do not claim to have consulted or exhausted an unavailable original document.
- Interpret “not overly casual” as polished everyday dressing appropriate to the activity, not automatically business suits, dress shoes, evening dresses, or occasionwear. Achieve refinement through fit, drape, fabric, neckline, waist definition, and deliberate styling. Consider varied categories such as structured blouses, fine-gauge knit tops, knit polos, waistcoats, tailored separates, pleated or wrap skirts, shirt dresses, and jumpsuits when suitable; these are options, not mandatory combinations. Explicit requests for business or event formality take precedence.
- Name concrete garments with their colors immediately attached, and relevant cuts, materials, and pattern descriptions. Different people should have distinct silhouettes and outfits rather than uniforms.

### Rug-derived clothing colors and patterns

- Internally identify the rug's dominant and secondary hue families, lightness, saturation, contrast, and pattern rhythm before choosing clothes. Derive a new clothing palette using adjacent/analogous hues or restrained complementary hues relative to those rug colors. Do not directly reuse the rug's exact main or accent shades as clothing colors, or reproduce its complete palette. Merely renaming the same color does not count as a new shade.
- Use perceptibly shifted hue, value, or saturation for an analogous relationship; temper complementary colors so clothing remains harmonious and does not overpower the rug. For a neutral rug, use its warm/cool undertone to select a compatible neighboring muted hue or a gently opposing hue, rather than claiming neutrals have a literal color-wheel complement. Explicit user-specified clothing colors override this default.
- Create a related but original garment motif from the rug's line rhythm, geometry, botanical language, or ornamental character. Change scale, spacing, orientation, or abstraction; never print a copy of the rug on the clothes. Usually concentrate the motif on one garment or small trim and keep the other pieces quiet. Honor explicit solid-color requests. Describe the actual pattern and colors briefly, not merely “rug-inspired clothing.”
- Coordinate garment colors and patterns together with the silhouette and scene. Do not default to ivory bottoms plus a pastel top, or any other repeated palette or garment formula across different rugs.

### Outfit variety check

- Compare against the latest three accessible outfit schemes for the same person composition in the conversation, including earlier prompts in the current batch. For different rugs, change both the garment-color pairing and at least two of garment categories/combination, cut/proportions, fabric treatment, and motif/placement. A new rug should not receive the same outfit with renamed colors.
- Keep choices seasonally and physically appropriate for the activity. Explicit outfit requests and deliberate character continuity override variation. Do not claim deduplication against unavailable conversation history.
- Before output, check that the new outfit is independently composed, its palette uses the required relationship without exact rug-color matching, its motif is related but not copied, and its formality matches the user's actual wording.

### Season and style
- Summer clothing is breathable and lightly layered; winter clothing is warm without automatically defaulting to black or gray. Do not add indoor bags or socks.
- Treat wardrobe directions as inspiration, never a fixed outfit template. Let the actual rug's palette and pattern scale guide the result.
- Persian/bohemian cues can inform restrained vintage shapes and trims; nature-inspired cues can inform natural fibers and relaxed shapes; modern abstract cues can inform clean or asymmetric cuts; botanical cues can inform a restrained floral or embroidered detail; craft-oriented cues can inform woven trim, patchwork, or visible seamwork; low-contrast patterns can inform gentle tonal checks or stripes. Do not require literal pattern matching or every listed cue.
- Maintain the requested room and activity. The parent SKILL.md controls rug placement, palette-only reference use, prompt language, and the integrated seven-paragraph output.
